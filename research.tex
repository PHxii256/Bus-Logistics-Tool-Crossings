\begin{abstract}
This paper addresses the limited transferability of existing School 
Bus Routing Problem (SBRP) formulations to developing countries. We 
introduce two key, student welfare focused, contributions: (1) a 
dynamic maximum ride time (DMRT) constraint, computed per student from 
geospatial OpenStreetMap data, replacing the policy based fixed maximum 
ride time (MRT) constraint common in literature involving developed 
countries, which is often absent in developing countries. Unlike fixed 
MRT, DMRT scales with each student's direct travel time, reducing the 
disparity between direct and assigned travel times while accommodating 
larger service areas; and (2) a refined student age-based stop 
selection process that enforces maximum walking distance (MWD) and 
street crossing constraints, accounting for limited pedestrian 
infrastructure such as crosswalks prevalent in developing countries. 
The proposed model is implemented using Adaptive Large Neighborhood 
Search (ALNS).
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
School Bus Routing Problem, Stop Selection, Developing Countries, ALNS
\end{keywords}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Transportation is a critical yet costly component of educational 
infrastructure \cite{c1}. The School Bus Routing Problem (SBRP) seeks 
to determine the most efficient way to transport students to and from 
school using a fleet of buses, and has been studied for over five 
decades since its formalization by Newton and Thomas \cite{c2}. 
Applying existing SBRP formulations to developing countries introduces 
unique challenges: the absence of standardized maximum ride time (MRT) 
policies, which are a common constraint in SBRP literature \cite{c3}, 
and the lack of formal pedestrian infrastructure such as designated 
crosswalks, making uncontrolled street crossing the systemic norm, a 
reality not captured in current stop selection models \cite{c4}. Stop 
selection determines which candidate bus stops should be used and 
assigns students to them, commonly subject to a maximum walking 
distance (MWD) or time (MWT) constraint \cite{c5}. These challenges 
are further compounded by the scarcity of highly detailed road network 
data in developing countries \cite{c4}.

This study makes two key contributions to the SBRP literature. First, 
we introduce a Dynamic Maximum Ride Time (DMRT) constraint that 
replaces the conventional fixed MRT \cite{c3} with a per-student 
threshold proportional to each student's direct travel time to school, 
balancing operational efficiency with equitable student welfare. 
Second, we propose modifications to the stop selection process 
\cite{c6} tailored to developing-country conditions, where uncontrolled 
street crossing is common. Synthetic crossings are introduced into the 
map data, and crossing permissions are assigned based on road class and 
student school stage as a proxy for age \cite{c4}. For example, 
residential streets are considered safe to cross in most instances, 
while tertiary roads are restricted for younger students. Crossing is 
prohibited entirely for students with physical disabilities \cite{c7}. 
MWT constraints are similarly differentiated by school stage and 
physical ability rather than applying a uniform threshold \cite{c1}.

The proposed formulation uses real-world road network data from 
OpenStreetMap (OSM) \cite{c19} and is solved using Adaptive Large 
Neighborhood Search (ALNS) \cite{c9}. OSM data is accessed and 
processed via the OSMnx library \cite{c8}. As no publicly available 
dataset combining real student locations with real road network data 
was found in the literature \cite{c4}, synthetic student addresses 
were generated around a real school in Cairo, Egypt, a dense urban 
setting representative of a developing country, to evaluate the 
proposed approach.

\section{RELATED WORKS}

The SBRP is a well-established combinatorial optimization problem and 
a specialized variant of the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) \cite{c10}. 
Desrosiers et al. \cite{c5} were among the first to formalize its 
structure, decomposing it into five interrelated sub-problems: data 
preparation, bus stop selection, route generation, school bell time 
adjustment, and route scheduling. Comprehensive reviews by Park and 
Kim \cite{c3} and Ellegood et al. \cite{c1} document the evolution of 
the field across these sub-problems, while a recent systematic review 
by Wang et al. \cite{c4} analyzing 127 peer-reviewed studies from 1970 
to 2024 confirms that route generation has consistently attracted the 
predominant share of scholarly attention, while stop selection remains 
comparatively underexplored, appearing in approximately one quarter of 
SBRP publications.

\subsection{Stop Selection}

The stop selection sub-problem entails choosing a subset of candidate 
bus stops and assigning students to them, subject to a maximum walking 
distance (MWD) or time (MWT) constraint. Despite its operational 
importance, it is frequently omitted or treated as a fixed parameter in 
the literature, with prevailing studies assuming stop locations as 
given inputs \cite{c4}. When stop selection is explicitly modeled, it 
is most commonly addressed as part of a location-allocation-routing 
(LAR) strategy, in which stops are first selected and students assigned 
before routes are generated \cite{c11}. Schittekat et al. \cite{c6} 
proposed metaheuristic approaches that jointly optimize stop selection 
and routing, demonstrating that treating the two sub-problems 
independently can yield sub-optimal solutions. Calvete et al. 
\cite{c12} developed a partial-allocation matheuristic that handles 
heterogeneous stop configurations, while Sciortino et al. \cite{c15} 
integrated heterogeneous fleet structures with stop selection in a 
single iterated local search framework, demonstrating both theoretical 
innovation and practical relevance across 20 real-world instances.

Safety considerations in stop selection have received increasing 
attention, with guidelines emphasizing hazards associated with walking 
to stops, including the absence of suitable footpaths and crossing 
points \cite{c4}. Parvasi et al. \cite{c13} proposed a bi-level 
optimization model for stop selection that incorporates demand 
outsourcing, while Bowerman et al. \cite{c14} addressed stop selection 
within a multi-objective urban routing framework. Nevertheless, safety 
constraints in existing formulations are predominantly modeled as 
uniform walking thresholds and do not differentiate by student age, 
physical ability, or road class. The MWT constraint, where it appears, 
is almost universally a single fixed value applied across all students 
regardless of their demographic characteristics \cite{c3}.

\subsection{Maximum Ride Time Constraints}

Vehicle capacity and maximum ride time (MRT) are the two most 
universally enforced constraints in the SBRP literature. According to 
Ellegood et al. \cite{c1}, approximately 35\% of reviewed SBRP 
publications include an MRT constraint. In Wales, for instance, a 
maximum journey time of 45 and 60 minutes is recommended for primary 
and secondary pupils respectively, and a maximum walking distance of 
1.6 km is considered reasonable \cite{c15}. In Malta, school transport 
is provided free of charge to students residing at least 1 km from 
school, with no formal MRT policy \cite{c15}. These examples 
illustrate that MRT values are policy-derived and jurisdiction-specific, 
and are almost exclusively associated with developed-country contexts.

In virtually all formulations, the MRT is implemented as a single 
fixed scalar value applied uniformly to all students \cite{c3}. This 
approach is geographically blind in that it fails to account for the 
spatial diversity of a school's catchment area. A student residing five 
minutes from school and one residing an hour away are held to the same 
absolute threshold, which can produce structurally inequitable outcomes 
in large service areas. Wang et al. \cite{c4} note the school time 
windows (STW) constraint used by L\'opez Santana and Romero Carvajal 
\cite{c16}, which ensures a student's ride time does not exceed twice 
the direct travel time from stop to school, as an attempt to introduce 
proportionality into ride time constraints. No formulation in the 
reviewed literature, however, computes a per-student dynamic ride time 
threshold based on real-world network travel data.

\subsection{Metaheuristic Solution Approaches}

Given the NP-hard nature of the SBRP \cite{c1}, metaheuristics 
dominate the solution landscape. Wang et al. \cite{c4} report that 
metaheuristic and hybrid approaches account for over 60\% of solution 
methods in recent literature, with Genetic Algorithms (GA) being the 
most widely adopted. Adaptive Large Neighbourhood Search (ALNS) has 
emerged as a particularly effective approach for complex SBRP 
sub-classes due to its adaptive selection of destroy-and-repair 
operators, which allow the algorithm to target specific constraint 
violations and escape deep local optima \cite{c9}. Sciortino et al. 
\cite{c15} demonstrated the effectiveness of iterated local search for 
joint stop-selection and routing across instances with up to 1,817 
potential stops and 757 students, comparing favorably against a 
memetic algorithm benchmark \cite{c17} across 100 instances. The 
integration of real-world geographic data has also improved model 
realism, with several studies replacing Euclidean distance metrics with 
road network travel times derived from OpenStreetMap \cite{c19}, 
accessed via tools such as OSMnx \cite{c8}.

\subsection{SBRP in Developing Countries and Research Gaps}

The overwhelming majority of SBRP formulations have been developed 
for and validated in developed-country contexts, where dense road 
networks, robust pedestrian infrastructure, and formal transportation 
policies can be assumed. Wang et al. \cite{c4} classify the service 
environment as a primary determinant of SBRP structure, noting that 
scholarly attention has been disproportionately concentrated on urban 
settings with standardized infrastructure, while contexts characterized 
by sparse networks and inconsistent pedestrian infrastructure remain 
largely unaddressed. The few exceptions that address non-standard 
infrastructure contexts tend to focus on rural routing under sparse 
network connectivity \cite{c18}, without engaging with the specific 
challenges of dense, informal urban environments where pedestrian 
crossing infrastructure is absent and MRT policies do not exist.

The reviewed literature reveals three persistent gaps directly 
motivating this work. First, the MRT constraint in virtually all 
formulations is a fixed, policy-derived value that is both unavailable 
in many developing countries and structurally inequitable across 
heterogeneous service areas. Second, stop selection processes rarely 
differentiate by student characteristics such as age or physical 
ability, and do not account for the absence of formal crossing 
infrastructure. Third, no existing formulation has been designed 
specifically for, or validated in, a dense urban developing-country 
context using real road network data. This paper addresses these gaps 
by introducing a Dynamic Maximum Ride Time (DMRT) constraint 
personalized to each student's direct OSM-derived travel time, an 
age-differentiated stop selection process incorporating road-class 
crossing constraints, and a synthetic-crossing mechanism for 
environments lacking formal pedestrian infrastructure, implemented and 
evaluated using ALNS on a real road network in Cairo, Egypt.

\section{PROBLEM DESCRIPTION AND FORMULATION}

The SBRP is solved on a directed road graph $G=(V,A)$ built from 
OpenStreetMap \cite{c19} driving edges, accessed via OSMnx \cite{c8}. 
Let $S$ be students, $R$ be bus routes, and $P$ be candidate pickup 
nodes generated from student-centered pedestrian reachability. In the 
implemented model, each route is school-anchored (pickup sequence 
ending at the school node), the bus fleet used is homogeneous (all 
buses have the same capacity) and students may remain unserved when no 
feasible insertion exists. Therefore, the optimization is a constrained 
\textit{service maximization} model, not a strict all-served 
minimization model \cite{c1}.

\subsection{The Objective Function}
We maximize a weighted score that prioritizes serving students, then 
compact routing, while penalizing long bus travel time and excessive 
student walking distance \cite{c14}:

\begin{equation}
\max F = w_s\!\sum_{s\in S} y_s - w_r\!\sum_{r\in R} z_r - 
\sum_{r\in R} T_r - \sum_{s\in S} \phi_s
\end{equation}

where $y_s\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether student $s$ is served, 
$z_r\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether route $r$ is active, $T_r$ is 
route travel time, and $\phi_s$ is the walking penalty for student 
$s$. The implementation uses $w_s=10000$ and $w_r=5000$, which 
ensures one additional served student dominates the activation of one 
additional bus \cite{c15}.

\subsection{Student Welfare Focused Constraints}
The SBRP transforms student welfare into geographic and temporal 
constraints with a hard-feasibility layer (candidate generation and 
insertion checks) and a soft-cost layer (walking penalty in the 
objective) \cite{c3}. Hard constraints include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{School-Stage Dependent Street Crossing:} For each 
    student $s$, the pedestrian exploration graph is filtered by 
    street crossing safety permissions defined for their school stage 
    and physical ability \cite{c7}.
    
    \item \textbf{School-Stage Dependent Walking Distance (MWD):} A 
    stop $p$ is a feasible candidate for student $s$ only if 
    pedestrian distance in the filtered graph satisfies 
    $d_{walk}(s,p)\le W_{stage(s)}$, and the student is physically 
    able \cite{c6}.
    
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Maximum Ride Time (DMRT):} To prevent 
    potential maximum ride time unfairness arising from the 
    geographical distribution of students \cite{c4}, we define a 
    feasibility corridor for the student dynamic maximum ride time 
    $T^{\max}_s$:
    \begin{equation}
    T^{\max}_s = \max\left(\tau_{\mathrm{min}},\min\left(k\,
    T^{direct}_s,\;T^{direct}_s+\tau_{\mathrm{add}}\right)\right)
    \end{equation}
    
    This logic establishes a ``feasibility corridor'' serving three 
    objectives:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{The Floor (Operational Efficiency):} Sets a 
        minimum baseline ride time that any student can expect 
        (e.g., 30 min) to ensure the system remains operationally 
        efficient without over-constraining students who live close 
        to school \cite{c3}.
        
        \item \textbf{The Multiplier ($k$):} Maintains a consistent 
        service quality ratio for the median student population, using 
        it as a multiple of direct travel time \cite{c16}.
        
        \item \textbf{The Additive Ceiling (Student Comfort):} 
        Imposes an absolute cap especially relevant for long-distance 
        commutes where the multiplicative factor $k\,T^{direct}_s$ 
        would otherwise yield an unacceptably large $T^{\max}_s$ 
        \cite{c1}.
    \end{enumerate}
    
    \item \textbf{Configurable Directional Enforcement:} Let 
    $T^{AM}_s$ and $T^{PM}_s$ denote morning and afternoon in-vehicle 
    times \cite{c15}. In strict directional mode, insertion requires 
    $T^{AM}_s\le T^{\max}_s$. In a bidirectional-lenient mode, 
    insertion is rejected only when both directions violate the cap:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{reject iff } \left(T^{AM}_s>T^{\max}_s\right) \wedge 
    \left(T^{PM}_s>T^{\max}_s\right)
    \end{equation}
    which handles cases where a student who boards the bus first in 
    the AM direction may also be dropped off first in the PM 
    direction, resulting in a shorter effective ride time.
    
    \item \textbf{Walking Penalty Layer:} Even among feasible 
    candidates, longer walks are penalized in the objective by 
    $\phi_s$, thereby discouraging unnecessary walking to bus stops 
    \cite{c14}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Operational Constraints}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Capacity:} For each route $r$, assigned students 
    satisfy $\sum_{s\in S_r}1 \le Q_r$ \cite{c15}.
    
    \item \textbf{School-Anchored Route Structure:} Each route is an 
    ordered pickup sequence terminating at the school node \cite{c5}.
    
    \item \textbf{Single Assignment for Served Students:} Each served 
    student is assigned to exactly one stop on one route. Unserved 
    students are allowed and reflected through a reduced objective 
    value \cite{c1}.
    
    \item \textbf{Insertion Feasibility:} Candidate insertions must 
    satisfy capacity and ride-time rules for both the inserted student 
    and previously assigned downstream students \cite{c9}.
\end{itemize}

\section{PROPOSED METHOD}

This section details the pipeline developed to solve the School Bus 
Routing Problem with a focus on student welfare in developing 
countries. The approach integrates geospatial data processing from 
OpenStreetMap \cite{c19} via OSMnx \cite{c8}, dynamic candidate stop 
generation based on pedestrian safety heuristics, and a metaheuristic 
optimizer (ALNS) \cite{c9}. We first outline the end-to-end pipeline 
before detailing the synthetic data generation, network augmentation 
strategies, school-stage and physical-ability based candidate bus stop 
selection, and the core routing algorithm.

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}
The implemented workflow follows four stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Data Generation and Graph Construction:} 
    Synthesize student populations via an annulus-based distribution 
    around the school (Algorithm~\ref{alg:synthetic_data_gen}), and 
    build a drive graph from OpenStreetMap \cite{c19}.
    
    \item \textbf{Pedestrian Graph Augmentation:} Inject synthetic 
    pedestrian crossings between opposite carriageways (when safety 
    predicates hold) to model informal crossing behavior present in 
    many developing countries, where formal pedestrian infrastructure 
    is absent \cite{c4}.
    
    \item \textbf{School-Stage-Tiered Candidate Stop Selection:} For 
    each student, perform safety-filtered pedestrian search to 
    enumerate reachable pickup nodes and select the most promising 
    subset of stops \cite{c6}.
    
    \item \textbf{ALNS Route Optimization:} Solve the 
    service-maximization objective with destroy and repair operators 
    and simulated-annealing acceptance, creating bus routes from the 
    set of candidate bus stops \cite{c9}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Synthetic Student Dataset Generation}
Due to privacy constraints and the lack of publicly available school 
registries in the studied region, student home locations are 
synthetically generated using a Gaussian annulus-based spatial model. 
Rather than distributing students uniformly, the method concentrates 
sampling in a ring around the school, which better reflects practical 
bus-catchment behavior.

In our workflow, the input provides a random seed and a boundary polygon. 
The polygon is first resolved into its enclosing bounding box, and the 
school-to-corner maximum distance of that box defines the annulus scale. 
Annulus parameters are then ratio-driven: 
$d_{\max}=R_{bbox}\cdot\text{bbox\_radius\_scale}$, 
$\mu_d=d_{\max}\cdot\text{peak\_ratio}$, and 
$\sigma_d=(d_{\max}-d_{\min})\cdot\text{sigma\_ratio}$. 
For each attempt, a bearing is sampled uniformly in $[0,2\pi)$, converted 
to latitude/longitude, snapped to the nearest drive node \cite{c8}, and 
validated using boundary, forbidden-highway, restricted-landuse, and 
residential-context checks.

Each accepted student is assigned a school stage from the configured stage 
distribution, an age from the stage-specific age band, and a disability 
flag sampled from a configured probability $\rho_{dis}$. The disability 
attribute is included to support downstream accessibility-aware constraints.

We construct an OSM-based graph \cite{c19} using OSMnx \cite{c8} and 
extract road classification data from network edges. These attributes 
are used to estimate speeds and calculate travel times for the 
proposed bus routes.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Annulus-Based Synthetic Student Dataset Generation}
\label{alg:synthetic_data_gen}
\KwIn{School center $(\phi_c,\lambda_c)$, students $N$, seed, polygon $P$, 
stage distribution $\pi$, ratio params $(\text{peak\_ratio},\text{sigma\_ratio},\text{bbox\_radius\_scale})$, 
$d_{\min}$, disability rate $\rho_{dis}$, attempt cap $A_{\max}$}
\KwOut{Student set $S$ with snapped coordinates, stage, age, disability}
Set random seeds from input\;
Resolve polygon $P$ to bounding box and compute enclosing radius $R_{bbox}$\;
Set $d_{\max}\leftarrow R_{bbox}\cdot\text{bbox\_radius\_scale}$\;
Set $\mu_d\leftarrow d_{\max}\cdot\text{peak\_ratio}$ and 
$\sigma_d\leftarrow(d_{\max}-d_{\min})\cdot\text{sigma\_ratio}$\;
Build validity filters (boundary, forbidden highways, restricted landuse, residential context)\;
$S\leftarrow\emptyset$\;
\For{$i\leftarrow1$ \KwTo $N$}{
    \For{$a\leftarrow1$ \KwTo $A_{\max}$}{
        Sample $d\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_d,\sigma_d)$ truncated to $[d_{\min},d_{\max}]$, 
        $\theta\sim\mathcal{U}(0,2\pi)$\;
        Convert $(d,\theta)$ to candidate point and snap to nearest drive node\;
        \If{candidate passes all validity filters}{
            Sample stage $g_i\sim\pi$, sample age from stage band, sample disability 
            $q_i\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho_{dis})$\;
            Append $s_i=(\phi_i,\lambda_i,g_i,\text{age}_i,q_i)$ to $S$; \textbf{break}\;
        }
    }
}
\Return{$S$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{School-Stage-Tiered Candidate Stop Selection}

Candidate stops are not predefined; they are generated dynamically 
from each student's location using pedestrian reachability under 
road-class safety rules. This approach departs from the prevailing 
convention in the SBRP literature, where candidate stops are typically 
provided as fixed inputs \cite{c4}, and instead treats stop generation 
as an integrated part of the optimization pipeline \cite{c6}.

For each student $s$, a bounded pedestrian search is conducted on 
the augmented walk graph, producing a set of reachable road-network 
nodes within the student's school-stage-dependent maximum walking 
distance $W_{stage(s)}$. The search is constrained by a 
school-stage safety matrix $\Pi$ that governs which road classes the 
student is permitted to cross.

Reachable nodes are then mapped to drive-graph nodes and scored 
according to four criteria: pedestrian safety, adjacent road 
speed (based on road class); the routing algorithm prefers to route busses through wider and faster roads, walking distance from the student's 
home node, and consolidation potential (the number of other students 
who share the same candidate stop, favoring stops that serve multiple 
students and thus improve vehicle utilization \cite{c15}). The 
top-$K$ candidates per student are retained and passed to the ALNS 
optimizer as the feasible insertion set for that student. This ensures that the optimizer only evaluates welfare-compliant insertions, eliminating the need for 
post-hoc feasibility filtering of stop assignments \cite{c12}.

This design is particularly important in the context 
of developing countries addressed by this paper. In the absence of formally designated stops and pedestrian crossings \cite{c19}, a purely road-network-based 
candidate set would either over-restrict reachability (by treating 
divided carriageways as impenetrable barriers) or under-restrict it 
(by ignoring crossing safety entirely). The synthetic crossing 
augmentation described in the following subsection resolves this 
tension by selectively enabling crossings where geometric and safety 
conditions are satisfied.

\subsection{Pedestrian Graph Augmentation via Synthetic Crossings}

OpenStreetMap \cite{c19} drive topology frequently represents divided 
roads as two opposite carriageways without a direct pedestrian edge 
between sides. This prevents modeling of informal street crossing 
(jaywalking) when candidate generation relies only on the driving 
network. To address this, we augment the driving network with 
synthetic crossing edges between opposite sides of eligible roads 
(typically tertiary and secondary classes) when geometric and safety 
checks are satisfied, producing an augmented pedestrian graph. The 
pedestrian graph is used exclusively for bus stop candidate 
generation; bus routing remains on the original drive graph 
\cite{c8}.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:candidate_synth} summarizes the integrated 
stop-candidate pipeline. It combines school-stage safety permissions 
with synthetic crossing augmentation before bounded pedestrian 
exploration and ranking to generate the feasible insertion set. The 
dominant runtime comes from bounded graph traversal on the augmented 
walk graph; with neighborhood-bounded search, practical behavior is 
near-linear in explored edges per student. This step is critical 
because it decides the feasible insertion set seen by the ALNS 
optimizer.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Candidate Bus Stop Generation with Synthetic Crossings}
\label{alg:candidate_synth}
\KwIn{Drive graph $G_d$, pedestrian graph $G_w$, student $s$, 
school-stage policy $\Pi$, mode config}
\KwOut{Ranked candidate stop set $C_s$}
Construct stage-filtered pedestrian permissions for $s$ from $\Pi$\;
Detect eligible opposite-carriageway pairs under geometry and 
road-class rules\;
Insert synthetic crossing edges into $G_w$ only when safety 
predicates are satisfied\;
Run bounded pedestrian search from student frontage/home node on 
augmented $G_w$\;
Map reachable pedestrian nodes to drive-graph stop nodes\;
Score and rank candidates (safety, adjacent road speed, walk 
distance, consolidation potential)\;
Truncate to top-$K$ mode-dependent candidates\;
\Return{$C_s$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{ALNS Design}

The optimizer uses Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS) 
\cite{c9} to solve the service-maximization objective. ALNS was 
selected over simpler metaheuristics due to its demonstrated 
effectiveness on large-scale routing problems with complex, 
heterogeneous constraints \cite{c4}, and its natural support for 
welfare-aware feasibility checks at the operator level. The 
framework employs roulette-wheel operator selection with adaptive 
weight updates \cite{c9}, allowing the algorithm to favor 
destroy-repair pairs that recently improved solution quality.

Destroy operators include random removal, worst-cost removal, 
relatedness (Shaw-style) removal \cite{c20}, sequence removal, and 
route-merge removal. Repair uses two operators: random-order best 
insertion and regret-based insertion \cite{c9}. The regret-based 
operator is particularly effective in this setting because it 
prioritizes students whose assignment options are most constrained 
by the DMRT and MWD welfare constraints, reducing the likelihood 
that hard-to-serve students are left unserved at the end of a repair 
pass.

The implementation includes a sparse-construction phase: when 
service coverage is still low, destroy is skipped and repair is 
biased to accelerate feasible assignment growth before stronger 
diversification begins. Acceptance follows a simulated annealing 
(SA) criterion \cite{c21} in the maximization setting, allowing 
occasional acceptance of worse solutions to escape local optima. 
Stopping is hybrid: no-improvement patience, freeze-based stopping 
when temperature becomes very low, and wall-clock budget termination 
(only after an iteration floor). Early-stop and freeze checks are 
additionally gated by a minimum-iterations threshold to prevent 
premature termination on large instances.

To eliminate excessive computation when an effective optimum is 
reached, the optimizer applies a polishing window: if all students 
are served and active fleet size is at most the theoretical minimum 
($\lceil |S| / C \rceil$), patience is tightened to 30 iterations. 
After the main loop, if all students are served but active fleet 
exceeds the theoretical minimum, an optional short merge-focused 
tail (route-merge destroy + regret repair) attempts additional fleet 
consolidation \cite{c15}. A final repair pass then attempts to 
insert any remaining unserved students.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{ALNS Framework Loop}
\label{alg:alns_loop}
\KwIn{Initial solution $x_0$, destroy set $\mathcal{D}$, repair 
set $\mathcal{R}$, temperature $T_0$, cooling $\gamma$, patience 
limit $P_{max}$, time budget $B$, iteration floor $I_{floor}$, 
freeze threshold $T_{freeze}$, freeze patience $P_{freeze}$, 
merge-tail iterations $M$}
\KwOut{Best solution $x^*$}
$x \leftarrow x_0$; $x^* \leftarrow x_0$; initialize operator 
weights and scores\;
$P_{current} \leftarrow P_{max}$; $stagnation \leftarrow 0$\;
\For{$it \leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $it_{max}$}{
    \If{$it > I_{floor}$ and elapsed wall-clock $\ge B$}{
        \textbf{break} \tcp*{Time budget stop}
    }
    Select destroy $d \in \mathcal{D}$ and repair $r \in \mathcal{R}$ 
    via roulette-wheel over adaptive weights\;
    $x' \leftarrow r(d(x))$\;
    \If{$f(x') > f(x)$}{
        $x \leftarrow x'$\;
    }
    \Else{
        Accept $x'$ with SA probability 
        $\exp((f(x')-f(x))/T)$\;
    }
    Update operator scores and weights from solution quality 
    signal\;
    \If{$f(x) > f(x^*)$}{
        $x^* \leftarrow x$\;
        $stagnation \leftarrow 0$\;
    }
    \Else{
        $stagnation \leftarrow stagnation + 1$\;
    }
    \If{all served in $x^*$ and active buses 
    $\le \lceil |S|/C \rceil$}{
        $P_{current} \leftarrow \min(P_{current}, 30)$ 
        \tcp*{Polishing window}
    }
    \If{$it \ge I_{floor}$ and $stagnation \ge P_{current}$}{
        \textbf{break} \tcp*{Early stop triggered}
    }
    \If{$it \ge I_{floor}$ and $T \le T_{freeze}$ and 
    $stagnation \ge P_{freeze}$}{
        \textbf{break} \tcp*{Frozen search stop}
    }
    $T \leftarrow \gamma T$\;
}
\If{merge-tail enabled and all served and active buses 
$> \lceil |S|/C \rceil$}{
    run $M$ merge-focused iterations under remaining budget\;
}
run one final repair pass for any unserved students\;
\Return{$x^*$}\;
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:alns_loop} presents the implemented outer search 
structure, including time-budget and freeze-aware stopping, the 
polishing window, and the post-loop consolidation and rescue phases. 
Each main ALNS iteration costs one destroy, one repair, and one 
acceptance decision; empirical runtime is dominated by insertion 
feasibility checks inside repair.

\subsection{Constraint Handling in Search}

Constraint handling is designed to operationalize the two core 
welfare contributions of this work \cite{c4}. First, stage-aware 
pedestrian safety is enforced before insertion: candidate stops are 
generated only from school-stage compliant walking reachability 
(distance and crossing permissions), with physical-ability 
restrictions applied in the pedestrian layer \cite{c7}. Second, 
ride-time fairness is enforced at insertion time through DMRT caps, 
so temporal feasibility is personalized rather than uniform \cite{c3}.

During insertion, temporal feasibility supports three configurable 
policy variants for deployment and comparison:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] DMRT strict AM enforcement,
    \item[(ii)] DMRT bidirectional leniency, where rejection occurs 
    only if both AM and PM exceed the personalized cap,
    \item[(iii)] fixed MRT \cite{c3}, enabling direct comparison and 
    analysis of the effects of each variant on student welfare, 
    service level, and operational efficiency.
\end{enumerate}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:insertion_eval} shows the feasibility evaluator 
used by repair operators. This evaluator is the main source of 
computational load during repair, because each candidate may trigger 
AM/PM cap checks plus downstream consistency checks for already 
assigned students.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Feasibility-Aware Insertion Evaluation}
\label{alg:insertion_eval}
\KwIn{Route $r$, student $s$, candidate stop $p\in C_s$, insertion 
index $i$, cache/matrix, cap parameters}
\KwOut{Valid insertion flag and penalized insertion cost}
\If{capacity would be violated}{reject}\;
Compute $T_s^{AM}$ and $T_s^{PM}$ after insertion\;
\If{hard MRT mode is enabled \cite{c3}}{
    \If{$T_s^{AM}>\mathrm{MRT}$ or $T_s^{PM}>\mathrm{MRT}$}
    {reject}\;
}
\Else{
    Compute personalized dynamic cap $T_s^{\max}$\;
    \If{strict directional mode and $T_s^{AM} > T_s^{\max}$}
    {reject}\;
    \If{bidirectional-lenient mode and $T_s^{AM} > T_s^{\max}$ 
    and $T_s^{PM} > T_s^{\max}$}{reject}\;
}
Re-check impacted existing students for post-insertion cap 
violations\;
Compute walking penalty $\phi_s$ for $s \rightarrow p$\;
\If{walking is infeasible}{reject}\;
\Return{valid, $(\text{detour delta} + \phi_s)$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\end{equation}
