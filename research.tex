\begin{abstract}
This paper addresses the limited transferability of existing School 
Bus Routing Problem (SBRP) formulations to developing countries. We 
introduce two key, student welfare focused, contributions: (1) a 
dynamic maximum ride time (DMRT) constraint, computed per student 
from geospatial OpenStreetMap data, replacing the policy-based fixed 
maximum ride time (MRT) constraint common in literature involving 
developed countries, which is often absent in developing countries. 
Unlike fixed MRT, DMRT scales with each student's direct travel time 
via an additive offset above each student’s direct travel time, bounded below by a minimum floor, which reduces the disparity between direct and assigned travel times while preserving feasibility across diverse geographic distributions. DMRT improved service rate by up to 14.6\% over MRT of 60 minutes and reduced mean ride-time unfairness by up to 29.01\% relative to MRT of 90 minutes; and (2) a 
refined student age-based stop selection process that enforces 
maximum walking distance (MWD) and street crossing constraints, 
accounting for limited pedestrian infrastructure such as crosswalks 
prevalent in developing countries. The proposed model is implemented 
using Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS).
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
School Bus Routing Problem, Stop Selection, Developing Countries, 
ALNS, Dynamic Maximum Ride Time
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Transportation is a critical yet costly component of educational 
infrastructure \cite{c1}. The School Bus Routing Problem (SBRP) seeks 
to determine the most efficient way to transport students to and from 
school using a fleet of buses, and has been studied for over five 
decades since its formalization by Newton and Thomas \cite{c2}. 
Applying existing SBRP formulations to developing countries introduces 
unique challenges: the absence of standardized maximum ride time (MRT) 
policies, which are a common constraint in SBRP literature \cite{c3}, 
and the lack of formal pedestrian infrastructure such as designated 
crosswalks, making uncontrolled street crossing the systemic norm, a 
reality not captured in current stop selection models. Stop 
selection determines which candidate bus stops should be used and 
assigns students to them, commonly subject to a maximum walking 
distance (MWD) or time (MWT) constraint \cite{c5}. These challenges 
are further compounded by the scarcity of highly detailed road network 
data in developing countries.

This study makes two key contributions to the SBRP literature. First, 
we introduce a Dynamic Maximum Ride Time (DMRT) constraint that 
replaces the conventional fixed MRT \cite{c3} with a per-student 
threshold defined as an additive offset above each student's direct 
travel time to school, bounded below by a minimum floor. This design 
simultaneously prevents disproportionately long ride times for 
students living close to school and avoids over-restricting the 
solution space for students living far from school, balancing 
operational efficiency with equitable student welfare. Second, we 
propose modifications to the stop selection process \cite{c6} 
tailored to developing-country conditions, where uncontrolled street 
crossing is common. Synthetic crossings are introduced into the map 
data, and crossing permissions are assigned based on road class and 
student school stage as a proxy for age. For example, 
residential streets are considered safe to cross in most instances, 
while tertiary roads are restricted for younger students. Crossing is 
prohibited entirely for students with moderate to severe disabilities. 
MWD constraints are similarly differentiated by school stage and 
 presence of disability rather than applying a uniform threshold \cite{c1}.

The proposed formulation uses real-world road network data from
OpenStreetMap (OSM) and is solved using Adaptive Large
Neighborhood Search (ALNS) \cite{c9}. OSM data is accessed and
processed via the OSMnx library created by Boeing \cite{c8}. As we could not find publicly available
datasets combining real student locations, with student demographic distribution and real road network data in developing countries \cite{c4}, synthetic student addresses
were generated around a real school in Cairo, Egypt, a dense urban
setting representative of a developing country, to evaluate the
proposed approach.

\section{RELATED WORKS}

The SBRP is a well-established combinatorial optimization problem and 
a specialized variant of the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) \cite{c10}. 
Desrosiers et al. \cite{c5} were among the first to formalize its 
structure, decomposing it into five interrelated sub-problems: data 
preparation, bus stop selection, route generation, school bell time 
adjustment, and route scheduling. Comprehensive reviews by Park and 
Kim \cite{c3} and Ellegood et al. \cite{c1} document the evolution of 
the field across these sub-problems, while a recent systematic review 
by Wang et al. \cite{c4} analyzing 127 peer-reviewed studies from 
1970 to 2024 confirms that route generation has consistently attracted 
the predominant share of scholarly attention, while stop selection 
remains comparatively underexplored, appearing in approximately one 
quarter of SBRP publications.

\subsection{Stop Selection}

The stop selection sub-problem entails choosing a subset of candidate 
bus stops and assigning students to them, subject to a maximum walking 
distance (MWD) or time (MWT) constraint. Despite its operational 
importance, it is frequently omitted or treated as a fixed parameter 
in the literature, with prevailing studies assuming stop locations as 
given inputs \cite{c4}. When stop selection is explicitly modeled, it 
is most commonly addressed as part of a location-allocation-routing 
(LAR) strategy, in which stops are first selected and students 
assigned before routes are generated \cite{c11}. Schittekat et al. 
\cite{c6} proposed metaheuristic approaches that jointly optimize 
stop selection and routing, demonstrating that treating the two 
sub-problems independently can yield sub-optimal solutions. Calvete 
et al. \cite{c12} developed a partial-allocation matheuristic that 
handles heterogeneous stop configurations, while Sciortino et al. 
\cite{c15} integrated heterogeneous fleet structures with stop 
selection in a single iterated local search framework, demonstrating 
both theoretical innovation and practical relevance across 20 
real-world instances.

Safety considerations in stop selection have received increasing 
attention, with guidelines emphasizing hazards associated with walking 
to stops, including the absence of suitable footpaths and crossing 
points \cite{c4}. Parvasi et al. \cite{c13} proposed a bi-level 
optimization model for stop selection that incorporates demand 
outsourcing, while Bowerman et al. \cite{c14} addressed stop 
selection within a multi-objective urban routing framework. 
Nevertheless, safety constraints in existing formulations are 
predominantly modeled as uniform walking thresholds and do not 
differentiate by student age,  presence of disability, or road class. The 
MWD constraint, where it appears, is almost universally a single 
fixed value applied across all students regardless of their 
demographic characteristics \cite{c3}.

\subsection{Maximum Ride Time Constraints}

Vehicle capacity and maximum ride time (MRT) are the two most 
universally enforced constraints in the SBRP literature. According 
to Ellegood et al. \cite{c1}, approximately 35\% of reviewed SBRP 
publications include an MRT constraint. In Wales, for instance, a 
maximum journey time of 45 and 60 minutes is recommended for primary 
and secondary pupils respectively, and a maximum walking distance of 
1.6 km is considered reasonable \cite{c15}. In Malta, school 
transport is provided free of charge to students residing at least 
1 km from school, with no formal MRT policy \cite{c15}. These 
examples illustrate that MRT values are policy-derived and 
jurisdiction-specific, and are almost exclusively associated with 
developed-country contexts.

In virtually all formulations, the MRT is implemented as a single 
fixed scalar value applied uniformly to all students \cite{c3}. 
This approach is geographically blind in that it fails to account 
for the spatial diversity of a school's catchment area. As 
illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:dmrt_corridor_60}, a fixed cap of 60 
minutes, a value commonly used in the SBRP literature, 
restricts the feasible insertion space for students whose direct 
travel time approaches or exceeds 60 minutes, rendering them 
unservable despite no proportional justification. The green 
Improved Service Region in Fig.~\ref{fig:dmrt_corridor_60} 
represents students who cannot be assigned any feasible route 
under a fixed MRT of 60 minutes but remain within the DMRT 
feasibility corridor, representing a direct gain in coverage. 
A natural response is to raise the fixed MRT to accommodate 
more distant students. However, as shown in 
Fig.~\ref{fig:dmrt_corridor}, increasing the cap for example to 90 minutes, similar to what Chen et al \cite{c21} used, to increase service to farther students,
introduces an Unfairness Region: students living close to 
school, with a short direct travel time such as a 10 minutes for example
,may legally be assigned a ride of up to 90 minutes under 
the higher fixed cap, a ratio that DMRT structurally prevents. 
Wang et al. \cite{c4} note the school time windows (STW) 
constraint used by L\'opez Santana and Romero Carvajal 
\cite{c16}, which ensures a student's ride time does not 
exceed twice the direct travel time from stop to school, as 
an attempt to introduce proportionality into ride time 
constraints. No formulation in the reviewed literature, 
however, defines a per-student ride time cap as an additive 
offset above direct travel time bounded by a minimum floor, 
as proposed in this paper.

\subsection{Metaheuristic Solution Approaches}

Given the NP-hard nature of the SBRP \cite{c1}, metaheuristics 
dominate the solution landscape. Wang et al. \cite{c4} report that 
metaheuristic and hybrid approaches account for over 60\% of 
solution methods in recent literature, with Genetic Algorithms (GA) 
being the most widely adopted. Adaptive Large Neighbourhood Search 
(ALNS) has emerged as a particularly effective approach for complex 
SBRP sub-classes due to its adaptive selection of destroy and repair 
operators, which allow the algorithm to target specific constraint 
violations and escape deep local optima especially with the help of Simulated Annealing (SA) \cite{c9}. Sciortino et al. 
\cite{c15} demonstrated the effectiveness of iterated local search 
for joint stop-selection and routing across instances with up to 
1,817 potential stops and 757 students, comparing favorably against 
a memetic algorithm benchmark \cite{c17} across 100 instances. 

\subsection{SBRP in Developing Countries and Research Gaps}

The overwhelming majority of SBRP formulations have been developed 
for and validated in developed-country contexts, where dense road 
networks, robust pedestrian infrastructure, and formal transportation 
policies can be assumed. Wang et al. \cite{c4} classify the service 
environment as a primary determinant of SBRP structure, noting that 
scholarly attention has been disproportionately concentrated on urban 
settings with standardized infrastructure, while contexts 
characterized by sparse networks and inconsistent pedestrian 
infrastructure remain largely unaddressed. The few exceptions that 
address non-standard infrastructure contexts tend to focus on rural 
routing under sparse network connectivity \cite{c18}, without 
engaging with the specific challenges of dense, informal urban 
environments where pedestrian crossing infrastructure is absent and 
MRT policies do not exist.

The reviewed literature reveals three persistent gaps directly 
motivating this work. First, the MRT constraint in virtually all 
formulations is a fixed, policy-derived value that is both 
unavailable in many developing countries and structurally inequitable: a low cap over-restricts distant 
students while a high cap creates disproportionate burdens for nearby 
students, as illustrated in Figs.~\ref{fig:dmrt_corridor_60} 
and~\ref{fig:dmrt_corridor}. Second, stop selection processes 
rarely differentiate by student characteristics such as age or 
 disability, and do not account for the absence of formal 
crossing infrastructure. Third, no existing formulation has been 
designed specifically for an urban 
developing country context using real road network data and informal crossing behavior. This paper 
addresses these gaps by introducing a DMRT constraint defined as an 
additive offset above each student's direct travel time 
bounded by a minimum floor, an age-differentiated stop selection 
process incorporating road-class crossing constraints, and a 
synthetic-crossing mechanism for environments lacking formal 
pedestrian infrastructure, implemented and evaluated using ALNS on 
a real road network in Cairo, Egypt.

\section{PROBLEM DESCRIPTION AND FORMULATION}

The SBRP is solved on a directed road graph $G=(V,A)$ built from 
OpenStreetMap driving edges, accessed via OSMnx \cite{c8}. 
Let $S$ be students, $R$ be bus routes, and $P$ be candidate pickup 
nodes generated from student-centered pedestrian reachability. In the 
implemented model, each route is school-anchored (pickup sequence 
ending at the school node), the bus fleet is homogeneous (all buses 
have the same capacity), and students may remain unserved when no 
feasible insertion exists. The optimization is therefore a constrained 
\textit{service maximization} model, not a strict all-served 
minimization model \cite{c1}.

\subsection{The Objective Function}

We maximize a weighted score that prioritizes serving students, then 
compact routing, while penalizing long bus travel time and excessive 
student walking distance \cite{c14}:

\begin{equation}
\max F = w_s\!\sum_{s\in S} y_s - w_r\!\sum_{r\in R} z_r - 
\sum_{r\in R} T_r - \sum_{s\in S} \phi_s
\end{equation}

where $y_s\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether student $s$ is served, 
$z_r\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether route $r$ is active, $T_r$ is 
route travel time, and $\phi_s$ is the walking penalty for student 
$s$. The implementation uses $w_s=10000$ and $w_r=5000$, which 
ensures one additional served student always dominates the activation 
of one additional bus in the objective.

\subsection{Student Welfare Focused Constraints}

The SBRP transforms student welfare into geographic and temporal 
constraints with a hard-feasibility layer (candidate generation and 
insertion checks) and an objective-cost layer (walking penalty in the 
objective). Hard constraints include:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{School-Stage Dependent Street Crossing:} For each 
student $s$, the pedestrian exploration graph is filtered by street 
crossing safety permissions defined for their school stage and 
 presence of disability.

\item \textbf{School-Stage Dependent Walking Distance (MWD):} A 
stop $p$ is a feasible candidate for student $s$ only if pedestrian 
distance in the filtered graph satisfies $d_{walk}(s,p) \le 
W_{stage(s)}$, and the student is physically able.

\item \textbf{Dynamic Maximum Ride Time (DMRT):} To prevent 
ride time unfairness arising from the geographical distribution of 
students, we define a per-student ride time cap 
$T^{\max}_s$ as:

\begin{equation}
T^{\max}_s = \max\!\left(\tau_{\min},\; T^{direct}_s + 
\tau_{add}\right)
\label{eq:dmrt}
\end{equation}

where $T^{direct}_s$ is the student's direct drive time from home 
to school on the road network, $\tau_{add}$ is a fixed additive 
offset representing the maximum acceptable detour above direct 
travel time, and $\tau_{\min}$ is a minimum floor that prevents 
over-restriction of students who live very close to school. As 
shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:dmrt_corridor_60} 
and~\ref{fig:dmrt_corridor}, this formulation simultaneously 
expands the feasible service space relative to a low fixed cap 
while preventing the inequitable ride time assignments that arise 
when a high fixed cap is used to compensate.

\item \textbf{Configurable Directional Enforcement:} Let 
$T^{AM}_s$ and $T^{PM}_s$ denote morning and afternoon in-vehicle 
times respectively \cite{c15}. In strict directional mode, 
insertion requires $T^{AM}_s \le T^{\max}_s$. In 
bidirectional-lenient mode, insertion is rejected only when both 
directions violate the cap:
\begin{equation}
\text{reject iff } \left(T^{AM}_s > T^{\max}_s\right) \wedge 
\left(T^{PM}_s > T^{\max}_s\right)
\end{equation}
which handles cases where a student who boards the bus first in 
the AM direction may also be dropped off first in the PM direction, 
resulting in a shorter effective ride time.

\item \textbf{Walking Penalty Layer:} Even among feasible 
candidates, longer walks are penalized in the objective by $\phi_s$, 
thereby discouraging unnecessary walking to bus stops \cite{c14}.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Operational Constraints}

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Capacity:} For each route $r$, assigned students 
satisfy $\sum_{s \in S_r} 1 \le Q_r$ \cite{c15}.

\item \textbf{School-Anchored Route Structure:} Each route is an 
ordered pickup sequence terminating at the school node \cite{c5}.

\item \textbf{Single Assignment for Served Students:} Each served 
student is assigned to exactly one stop on one route. Unserved 
students are permitted and reflected through a reduced objective 
value \cite{c1}.

\item \textbf{Insertion Feasibility:} Candidate insertions must 
satisfy capacity and ride-time rules for both the inserted student 
and all previously assigned downstream students \cite{c9}.

\end{itemize}

\section{PROPOSED METHOD}

This section details the pipeline developed to solve the SBRP with 
a focus on student welfare in developing countries. The approach 
integrates geospatial data processing from OpenStreetMap 
via OSMnx, dynamic candidate stop generation based on 
pedestrian safety heuristics, and a metaheuristic optimizer 
(ALNS) \cite{c9}. We first outline the end-to-end pipeline before 
detailing the synthetic data generation, network augmentation 
strategies, school-stage and physical-ability based candidate bus 
stop selection, and the core routing algorithm.

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}

The implemented workflow follows four stages:

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Synthetic Data Generation and Graph Construction:} 
Synthesize student populations via an annulus-based distribution 
around the school (Algorithm~\ref{alg:synthetic_data_gen}), and 
build a drive graph from OpenStreetMap.

\item \textbf{Pedestrian Graph Augmentation:} Inject synthetic 
pedestrian crossings between opposite carriageways (when safety 
predicates hold) to model informal crossing behavior present in 
many developing countries, where formal pedestrian infrastructure 
is absent.

\item \textbf{School-Stage-Tiered Candidate Stop Selection:} For 
each student, perform safety-filtered pedestrian search to 
enumerate reachable pickup nodes and select the most promising 
subset of stops.

\item \textbf{ALNS Route Optimization:} Solve the 
service-maximization objective with destroy and repair operators 
and simulated-annealing acceptance, creating bus routes from the 
set of candidate bus stops \cite{c9}.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Synthetic Student Dataset Generation}

Due to privacy constraints and the lack of publicly available school 
registries in the studied region, student home locations are 
synthetically generated using a Gaussian annulus-based spatial 
model. Rather than distributing students uniformly across the map, 
this approach more accurately models real-world school catchment 
areas, where enrollment density is highest at an intermediate 
distance from school and tapers off both for very distant students 
(lower enrollment likelihood) and for students living within easy 
walking distance (who would not require bus transport).

In our workflow, the input provides a random seed and a boundary 
polygon. The polygon is first resolved into its enclosing bounding 
box, and the school-to-corner maximum distance of that box defines 
the annulus scale. Annulus parameters are then ratio-driven: 
$d_{\max}=R_{bbox}\cdot\text{bbox\_radius\_scale}$, 
$\mu_d=d_{\max}\cdot\text{peak\_ratio}$, and 
$\sigma_d=(d_{\max}-d_{\min})\cdot\text{sigma\_ratio}$. 
For each attempt, a bearing is sampled uniformly in $[0,2\pi)$, 
converted to latitude/longitude, snapped to the nearest drive node, and validated using boundary, forbidden-highway, 
restricted-landuse, and residential-context checks.

Each generated student is assigned a school stage from the 
configured stage distribution, an age from the stage-specific age 
band, and a disability flag sampled from a configured probability 
$\rho_{dis}$. The disability attribute is included to support 
downstream accessibility-aware constraints. The direct travel time 
$T^{direct}_s$ for each student, which is the primary input to the 
DMRT formula (Eq.~\ref{eq:dmrt}), is computed as the 
shortest-path drive time from the student's snapped home node to 
the school node on the OSM-derived road graph.

We construct an OSM-based graph using OSMnx
and extract road classification data from network edges. These 
attributes are used to estimate speeds and calculate travel times 
for the proposed bus routes.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Annulus-Based Synthetic Student Dataset Generation}
\label{alg:synthetic_data_gen}
\KwIn{School center $(\phi_c,\lambda_c)$, students $N$, seed, 
polygon $P$, stage distribution $\pi$, ratio params 
$(\text{peak\_ratio},\text{sigma\_ratio},\text{bbox\_radius\_scale})$, 
$d_{\min}$, disability rate $\rho_{dis}$, attempt cap $A_{\max}$}
\KwOut{Student set $S$ with snapped coordinates, stage, age, 
disability, and $T^{direct}_s$}
Set random seeds from input\;
Resolve polygon $P$ to bounding box and compute enclosing radius 
$R_{bbox}$\;
Set $d_{\max}\leftarrow R_{bbox}\cdot\text{bbox\_radius\_scale}$\;
Set $\mu_d\leftarrow d_{\max}\cdot\text{peak\_ratio}$ and 
$\sigma_d\leftarrow(d_{\max}-d_{\min})\cdot\text{sigma\_ratio}$\;
Build validity filters (boundary, forbidden highways, restricted 
landuse, residential context)\;
$S\leftarrow\emptyset$\;
\For{$i\leftarrow1$ \KwTo $N$}{
    \For{$a\leftarrow1$ \KwTo $A_{\max}$}{
        Sample $d\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_d,\sigma_d)$ truncated to 
        $[d_{\min},d_{\max}]$, $\theta\sim\mathcal{U}(0,2\pi)$\;
        Convert $(d,\theta)$ to candidate point and snap to 
        nearest drive node\;
        \If{candidate passes all validity filters}{
            Sample stage $g_i\sim\pi$, age from stage band, 
            disability $q_i\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho_{dis})$\;
            Compute $T^{direct}_{s_i}$ as shortest-path drive 
            time to school node\;
            Append $s_i=(\phi_i,\lambda_i,g_i,\text{age}_i,
            q_i,T^{direct}_{s_i})$ to $S$\;
            \textbf{break}\;
        }
    }
}
\Return{$S$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{School-Stage-Tiered Candidate Stop Selection}

Candidate stops are not predefined; they are generated dynamically 
from each student's location using pedestrian reachability under 
road-class safety rules. This approach departs from the prevailing 
convention in the SBRP literature, where candidate stops are 
typically provided as fixed inputs \cite{c4}, and instead treats 
stop generation as an integrated part of the optimization 
pipeline \cite{c6}.

For each student $s$, a bounded pedestrian search is conducted on 
the augmented walk graph, producing a set of reachable road-network 
nodes within the student's school-stage-dependent maximum walking 
distance $W_{stage(s)}$. The search is constrained by a 
school-stage safety matrix $\Pi$ that governs which road classes 
the student is permitted to cross.

Reachable nodes are then mapped to drive-graph nodes and scored 
according to four criteria: pedestrian safety and adjacent road 
speed based on road class (the routing algorithm prefers to route 
buses through wider and faster roads), walking distance from the 
student's home node, and consolidation potential (the number of 
other students who share the same candidate stop, favoring stops 
that serve multiple students and thus improve vehicle 
utilization \cite{c15}). The top-$K$ candidates per student are 
retained and passed to the ALNS optimizer as the feasible insertion 
set for that student. This ensures that the optimizer only 
evaluates welfare-compliant insertions, eliminating the need for 
post-hoc feasibility filtering of stop assignments \cite{c12}.

This design is particularly important in the developing-country
context addressed by this paper. In the absence of formally
designated stops and pedestrian crossings, a purely
road-network-based candidate set would either over-restrict
reachability (by treating divided carriageways as impenetrable
barriers) or under-restrict it (by ignoring crossing safety
entirely). The synthetic crossing augmentation described in the
following subsection resolves this tension by selectively enabling
crossings where geometric and safety conditions are satisfied.

\subsection{Pedestrian Graph Augmentation via Synthetic Crossings}

OpenStreetMap drive topology frequently represents
divided roads as two opposite carriageways without a direct
pedestrian edge between sides. This prevents modeling of informal
street crossing (jaywalking) when candidate generation relies only
on the driving network. To address this, we augment the driving
network with synthetic crossing edges between opposite sides of
eligible roads (typically tertiary and secondary classes) when
geometric and safety checks are satisfied, producing an augmented
pedestrian graph. The pedestrian graph is used exclusively for bus
stop candidate generation; bus routing remains on the original
drive graph.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:candidate_synth} summarizes the integrated 
stop-candidate pipeline. It combines school-stage safety 
permissions with synthetic crossing augmentation before bounded 
pedestrian exploration and ranking to generate the feasible 
insertion set. The dominant runtime comes from bounded graph 
traversal on the augmented walk graph; with neighborhood-bounded 
search, practical behavior is near-linear in explored edges per 
student. This step is critical because it determines the feasible 
insertion set seen by the ALNS optimizer.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Candidate Bus Stop Generation with Synthetic Crossings}
\label{alg:candidate_synth}
\KwIn{Drive graph $G_d$, pedestrian graph $G_w$, student $s$, 
school-stage policy $\Pi$, mode config}
\KwOut{Ranked candidate stop set $C_s$}
Construct stage-filtered pedestrian permissions for $s$ from 
$\Pi$\;
Detect eligible opposite-carriageway pairs using road names under geometry and 
road-class rules\;
Insert synthetic crossing edges into $G_w$\;
Run bounded pedestrian search from student frontage/home node on 
augmented $G_w$\;
Map reachable pedestrian nodes to drive-graph stop nodes\;
Score and rank candidates (safety, adjacent road speed, walk 
distance, consolidation potential)\;
Truncate to top-$K$ candidates\;
\Return{$C_s$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{ALNS Design}

The optimizer uses Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS) 
\cite{c9} to solve the service-maximization objective. ALNS was 
selected over simpler metaheuristics due to its demonstrated 
effectiveness on large-scale routing problems \cite{c4}, and its natural support for 
welfare-aware feasibility checks at the operator level. The 
framework employs roulette-wheel operator selection with adaptive 
weight updates \cite{c9}, allowing the algorithm to favor 
destroy-repair pairs that recently improved solution quality.

Destroy operators include random removal, worst-cost removal,
relatedness (Shaw-style) removal \cite{c19}, sequence removal, and
route-merge removal. Repair uses two operators: random-order best
insertion and regret-based insertion \cite{c9}. The regret-based
operator is particularly effective in this setting because it
prioritizes students whose assignment options are most constrained
by the DMRT cap and MWD welfare constraints, reducing the
likelihood that hard-to-serve students are left unserved at the
end of a repair pass. The selected destroy-repair portfolio is also 
aligned with the operator-ranking evidence reported by Voigt 
\cite{c22}, which supports sequence-based removals and 
foresight-based insertion while maintaining a diversified set of 
operators for robustness.

The implementation includes a sparse-construction phase: when 
service coverage is still low, destroy is skipped and repair is 
biased to accelerate feasible assignment growth before stronger 
diversification begins. Acceptance follows a simulated annealing
(SA) criterion \cite{c20} in the maximization setting, allowing
occasional acceptance of worse solutions to escape local optima. 
Stopping is hybrid: no-improvement patience, freeze-based stopping 
when temperature becomes very low, and wall-clock budget termination 
(only after an iteration floor). Early-stop and freeze checks are 
additionally gated by a minimum-iterations threshold to prevent 
premature termination on large instances.

To eliminate excessive computation when an effective optimum is 
reached, the optimizer applies a polishing window: if all students 
are served and active fleet size is at most the theoretical minimum 
($\lceil |S| / C \rceil$), patience is tightened to 30 iterations. 
After the main loop, if all students are served but active fleet 
exceeds the theoretical minimum, an optional short merge-focused 
tail (route-merge destroy + regret repair) attempts additional 
fleet consolidation \cite{c15}. A final repair pass then attempts 
to insert any remaining unserved students.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{ALNS Framework Loop}
\label{alg:alns_loop}
\KwIn{Initial solution $x_0$, destroy set $\mathcal{D}$, repair 
set $\mathcal{R}$, temperature $T_0$, cooling $\gamma$, patience 
limit $P_{max}$, time budget $B$, iteration floor $I_{floor}$, 
freeze threshold $T_{freeze}$, freeze patience $P_{freeze}$, 
merge-tail iterations $M$}
\KwOut{Best solution $x^*$}
$x \leftarrow x_0$; $x^* \leftarrow x_0$; initialize operator 
weights and scores\;
$P_{current} \leftarrow P_{max}$; $stagnation \leftarrow 0$\;
\For{$it \leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $it_{max}$}{
    \If{$it > I_{floor}$ and elapsed wall-clock $\ge B$}{
        \textbf{break} \tcp*{Time budget stop}
    }
    Select destroy $d \in \mathcal{D}$ and repair 
    $r \in \mathcal{R}$ via roulette-wheel over adaptive 
    weights\;
    $x' \leftarrow r(d(x))$\;
    \If{$f(x') > f(x)$}{
        $x \leftarrow x'$\;
    }
    \Else{
        Accept $x'$ with SA probability 
        $\exp((f(x')-f(x))/T)$\;
    }
    Update operator scores and weights from solution quality 
    signal\;
    \If{$f(x) > f(x^*)$}{
        $x^* \leftarrow x$\;
        $stagnation \leftarrow 0$\;
    }
    \Else{
        $stagnation \leftarrow stagnation + 1$\;
    }
    \If{all served in $x^*$ and active buses 
    $\le \lceil |S|/C \rceil$}{
        $P_{current} \leftarrow \min(P_{current}, 30)$ 
        \tcp*{Polishing window}
    }
    \If{$it \ge I_{floor}$ and $stagnation \ge P_{current}$}{
        \textbf{break} \tcp*{Early stop triggered}
    }
    \If{$it \ge I_{floor}$ and $T \le T_{freeze}$ and 
    $stagnation \ge P_{freeze}$}{
        \textbf{break} \tcp*{Frozen search stop}
    }
    $T \leftarrow \gamma T$\;
}
\If{merge-tail enabled and all served and active buses 
$> \lceil |S|/C \rceil$}{
    run $M$ merge-focused iterations under remaining budget\;
}
run one final repair pass for any unserved students\;
\Return{$x^*$}\;
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:alns_loop} presents the implemented outer 
search structure, including time-budget and freeze-aware stopping, 
the polishing window, and the post-loop consolidation and rescue 
phases. Each main ALNS iteration costs one destroy, one repair, 
and one acceptance decision; empirical runtime is dominated by 
insertion feasibility checks inside repair.

\subsection{Constraint Handling in Search}

Constraint handling is designed to operationalize the two core 
welfare contributions of this work. First, stage-aware 
pedestrian safety is enforced before insertion: candidate stops 
are generated only from school-stage compliant walking 
reachability (distance and crossing permissions), with 
physical-ability restrictions applied in the pedestrian 
layer. Second, ride-time fairness is enforced at 
insertion time through the DMRT cap $T^{\max}_s$ 
(Eq.~\ref{eq:dmrt}), so temporal feasibility is personalized 
to each student's direct travel time rather than uniform.

During insertion, temporal feasibility supports three configurable 
policy variants for deployment and comparison:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] DMRT strict AM enforcement: reject if 
    $T^{AM}_s > T^{\max}_s$.
    \item[(ii)] DMRT bidirectional leniency: reject only if both 
    $T^{AM}_s > T^{\max}_s$ and $T^{PM}_s > T^{\max}_s$.
    \item[(iii)] Fixed MRT \cite{c3}: a uniform cap applied to 
    all students, enabling direct comparison and analysis of the 
    effects of each variant on student welfare, service level, 
    and operational efficiency.
\end{enumerate}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:insertion_eval} shows the feasibility evaluator 
used by repair operators. This evaluator is the main source of 
computational load during repair, because each candidate may 
trigger DMRT cap checks plus downstream consistency checks for 
already assigned students.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Feasibility-Aware Insertion Evaluation}
\label{alg:insertion_eval}
\KwIn{Route $r$, student $s$, candidate stop $p \in C_s$, 
insertion index $i$, cache/matrix, cap parameters 
$(\tau_{\min}, \tau_{add})$}
\KwOut{Valid insertion flag and penalized insertion cost}
\If{capacity would be violated}{reject\;}
Compute $T_s^{AM}$ and $T_s^{PM}$ after insertion\;
\If{hard MRT mode is enabled \cite{c3}}{
    \If{$T_s^{AM} > \mathrm{MRT}$ or 
    $T_s^{PM} > \mathrm{MRT}$}{reject\;}
}
\Else{
    Compute personalized cap 
    $T_s^{\max} \leftarrow \max(\tau_{\min},\; 
    T^{direct}_s + \tau_{add})$\;
    \If{strict mode and $T_s^{AM} > T_s^{\max}$}{reject\;}
    \If{lenient mode and $T_s^{AM} > T_s^{\max}$ and 
    $T_s^{PM} > T_s^{\max}$}{reject\;}
}
Re-check impacted existing students for post-insertion cap 
violations\;
Compute walking penalty $\phi_s$ for $s \rightarrow p$\;
\If{walking is infeasible}{reject\;}
\Return{valid, $(\text{detour delta} + \phi_s)$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Let $S^{served}$ denote the set of served students, $R^{act}$ 
the set of active routes, $Q_r$ the bus capacity on route $r$, 
$L_r$ the assigned student load on route $r$, and $p_s$ the 
stop assigned to student $s$. Let $S^{allow}$ denote students
eligible to cross under the policy (middle/high school and not
disabled), $S^{usedX}$ denote students who actually use at least one
synthetic crossing in their final assigned walk path, and
$S^{consX}$ denote students whose candidate-generation BFS traverses
at least one synthetic crossing edge. Let $W_s$ denote the maximum
allowed walking distance for student $s$, $S^{walk+}=\{s\in S^{served}:W_s>0\}$,
and $d_{walk}(s,p_s)$ denote the assigned walking distance from $s$
to stop $p_s$.

\subsubsection{Service and Coverage}
\begin{equation}
\text{ServiceRate} = \frac{|S^{served}|}{|S|}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{ActiveRoutes} = |R^{act}|
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Operational Efficiency}
\begin{equation}
\text{TotalRouteTime} = \sum_{r \in R^{act}} T_r
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{AvgRouteTime} = \frac{1}{|R^{act}|} \sum_{r \in R^{act}} T_r
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{FleetUtilization} = \frac{1}{|R^{act}|} 
\sum_{r \in R^{act}} \frac{L_r}{Q_r}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Ride Time Fairness}

The following metrics directly evaluate the equity contribution 
of DMRT by measuring how assigned ride times relate to each 
student's direct travel time. A fair policy minimizes both the 
mean ratio and its variance across students.

\begin{equation}
\text{RideRatioMean} = \frac{1}{|S^{served}|} 
\sum_{s \in S^{served}} \frac{T_s^{assigned}}{T_s^{direct}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{RideRatioStd} = \mathrm{Std}
\!\left(\frac{T_s^{assigned}}{T_s^{direct}}
\right)_{\!s \in S^{served}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{RideRatioMax} = \max_{s \in S^{served}} 
\frac{T_s^{assigned}}{T_s^{direct}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Stop Selection Metrics}

To evaluate stop-assignment behavior under walking and crossing
constraints, we report one walking metric and two crossing metrics.
The term $\frac{1}{|S^{walk+}|}$ is the averaging factor, where
$|S^{walk+}|$ is the number of served students with a positive
walking allowance ($W_s>0$). Likewise, $|S^{usedX}|$ and
$|S^{consX}|$ denote the cardinalities of the sets of students who
used crossings in final assignments and considered crossings during
BFS exploration, respectively.

\begin{equation}
\text{AllowedMeanWalkingDist} =
\frac{1}{|S^{walk+}|}
\sum_{s\in S^{walk+}} d_{walk}(s,p_s)
\end{equation}

The crossing metrics are normalized by the policy-eligible population
$|S^{allow}|$:

\begin{equation}
\text{UsedCrossing} = 100 \cdot 
\frac{|S^{usedX} \cap S^{allow}|}{|S^{allow}|}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{ConsideredCrossing} = 100 \cdot 
\frac{|S^{consX} \cap S^{allow}|}{|S^{allow}|}
\end{equation}

UsedCrossing captures realized crossing behavior in final assignments,
while ConsideredCrossing captures exploration-time crossing behavior
during bounded pedestrian BFS.

\subsubsection{Computational Performance}

Let $M$ denote the set of comparison modes and $\mathcal{I}_n$ the set
of runs at instance size $n=|S|$.

\begin{equation}
\text{RuntimeSeconds}(m) = t^{end}_m - t^{start}_m, \quad m \in M
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\text{ExecutedIterations}(m) = I^{exec}_m, \quad m \in M
\end{equation}

For per-run reporting (as in Table~\ref{tab:eval_i1}), we use the
terminal stop reason directly:

\begin{equation}
\text{StopReason}(i,m)=\rho_{i,m},\quad i\in\mathcal{I}_n,\; m\in M
\end{equation}

where $\rho_{i,m}$ is the terminal ALNS stop reason for run $i$ in mode $m$
(e.g., early-stop, freeze, time-budget). Runtime and iteration
counts are reported per mode and summarized by instance size.

\subsection{Comparison Modes}

To isolate the effect of the ride-time cap policy, the four ride time constraints runs below use identical candidate stop sets, ALNS operators and 
configuration, objective weights, and random seeds. The only 
component that differs across modes is the ride-time feasibility 
logic applied during insertion evaluation 
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:insertion_eval}).

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Fixed MRT (60 and 90 minutes)}: A single fixed ride-time cap 
of either 60 or 90 minutes is applied uniformly to all students, 
regardless of their proximity to school. This replicates the 
conventional constraint used in the SBRP literature \cite{c3}.

\item \textbf{DMRT Strict}: The personalized cap 
$T_s^{\max} = \max(\tau_{\min}, T^{direct}_s + \tau_{add})$ is 
enforced on the AM direction only. Insertion is rejected if 
$T_s^{AM} > T_s^{\max}$.

\item \textbf{DMRT Bidirectional Lenient}: The personalized cap 
is enforced jointly across both directions. Insertion is rejected 
only if both $T_s^{AM} > T_s^{\max}$ and $T_s^{PM} > T_s^{\max}$.

\end{itemize}

\section{RESULTS AND DISCUSSION}

This section evaluates the proposed DMRT-based formulation against
fixed MRT baselines under identical routing and search settings.
The analysis is organized into four parts: (A) service coverage and
operational efficiency, (B) stop-selection behavior,
(C) ride-time fairness, and (D) ALNS convergence and runtime behavior.
Together, these results
assess whether the proposed welfare-focused constraints improve equity
without sacrificing practical service levels or computational efficiency.

\subsection{Service Coverage and Operational Efficiency}

Table~\ref{tab:eval_i1} reports per-seed outcomes for the four
ride-time policies, while
Fig.~\ref{fig:service_prob_direct_time_i1} shows how service
probability varies with direct home-to-school travel time.
Aggregating across the three seeds, DMRT Lenient and DMRT Strict
achieve mean service rates of 0.680 and 0.690 respectively,
substantially higher than MRT 60 (0.602), while remaining below
MRT 90 (0.787). This confirms the expected policy trade-off:
a strict fixed cap under-serves distant students, whereas a very
high fixed cap maximizes coverage at the expense of substantially
longer rides.

Operationally, MRT 60 yields the lowest mean total route time
(755.32 min), but this is coupled with the lowest service. DMRT
Strict and DMRT Lenient increase total route time to 792.91 and
786.66 min respectively, while recovering about 7.8--8.8 percentage
points of service relative to MRT 60. MRT 90 produces the highest
total route time (1014.96 min) and the highest average route time,
indicating that its coverage gain is obtained by allowing materially
longer route detours. Figure~\ref{fig:service_prob_direct_time_i1}
is consistent with this pattern: DMRT curves dominate MRT 60 in the
mid-to-high direct-time bins, showing that DMRT mainly improves
inclusion for geographically distant students.

\subsection{Stop Selection Behavior}

Table~\ref{tab:stop_metrics_i1} reports the stop-selection metrics aggregated over seeds: Allowed Mean Walking Distance,
Used Crossing, and Considered Crossing. These metrics complement
service and fairness results by quantifying how the policy-constrained
pedestrian layer behaves in both final assignments and candidate
exploration.

In our framework, synthetic crossings are introduced specifically to
model informal street crossing behavior commonly observed in
developing countries, where formal pedestrian
infrastructure is limited. Accordingly, these metrics provide direct
evidence of how that modeled crossing mechanism is performed in
candidate exploration and in final assignments.

Allowed Mean Walking Distance summarizes the realized walking burden for
served students with a positive walking allowance. Used Crossing captures realized
crossing behavior in final assigned paths, while Considered Crossing
captures exploration-time exposure to synthetic crossings during
bounded BFS.

Because all comparison modes share the same crossing policy and
candidate-generation mechanism, differences in these metrics mainly
reflect indirect effects from mode-dependent service composition
(which students are ultimately served) and variation in synthetic student location
distribution across seeds. This section
therefore links route-level ride-time policy decisions to
pedestrian-access outcomes. Figure~\ref{fig:crossing_distribution}

largest RideRatioMax on average (15.49), whereas DMRT policies reduce
this value (11.87 for DMRT Strict and 11.25 for DMRT Lenient).
MRT 60 has the lowest mean maximum ratio (9.55), but this should
be interpreted together with its much lower service rate; many
hard to serve distant students are not served under MRT 60, which
can mechanically suppress observed unfairness among only the served
subset. Therefore, the combined evidence supports DMRT as an
improved service-fairness compromise over MRT 60, while MRT 90
remains the highest-coverage but highest-burden policy.

The computational metrics in Table~\ref{tab:eval_i1} indicate stable

\section{CONCLUSION}
This paper presented a student welfare-focused formulation of the
School Bus Routing Problem tailored to developing-country contexts,
where fixed policy MRT values and formal pedestrian infrastructure
are often unavailable. The proposed approach combines two core
mechanisms: (i) a Dynamic Maximum Ride Time (DMRT) cap,
$T_s^{\max}=\max(\tau_{\min},T_s^{direct}+\tau_{add})$, and
(ii) stage-aware stop selection with road-class crossing constraints
and synthetic crossing augmentation.

Experimental results on Cairo-based synthetic instances show that DMRT
improves service coverage relative to MRT 60 while preserving a
competitive fairness profile. On tested the instance, both DMRT variants
outperform MRT 60 in service, with DMRT Strict reaching 0.690 versus
0.602 for MRT 60. MRT 90 achieves the highest service (0.787), but at
substantially higher total route time and runtime, and higher unfairness when it comes to mean and max ride ratios. Fairness metrics
show that MRT 60 has the lowest mean ride ratio among served
students (2.203), while DMRT offers a better balance between service and mean ride ratio (2.219) for DMRT Lenient and (2.519) for DMRT Strict. Runtime analysis
indicates that DMRT Strict is the fastest mode on average, while MRT 90
is consistently the slowest.

Quantitatively, compared with MRT 60, DMRT Strict and DMRT Lenient increased service by 14.6\% and 13.0\%, respectively. However, these service gains came with higher ride-time unfairness relative to MRT 60: RideRatioMean increased by 14.34\% and 0.73\%, and RideRatioMax increased by 24.29\% and 17.80\%, respectively. In contrast, compared with MRT 90, both DMRT variants reduced RideRatioMean by 19.42\% and 29.01\%, and RideRatioMax by 23.37\% and 27.37\%, respectively.

Overall, the findings support DMRT as a practical and equitable
ride-time policy for developing countries, or any country lacking a fixed maximum student ride-time policy.

\section{LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK}
Figure~\ref{fig:crossing_distribution} highlights a key
methodological limitation: we were unable to produce and measure the effects of
synthetic crossings on solution quality, and we believe it is due to two main factors.

The first factor is the current strategy used to connect opposite
sides of a divided street. The present algorithm tries to pair two streets of the same name with opposite driving directions,
which works well on some streets, but it does not work well on roundabouts, medians, and one-way streets regardless of road class.

The second factor, as could be inferred from Fig.~\ref{fig:crossing_distribution}, is that there are relatively few students close to many of the aforementioned two-way streets,
and among those students there are even fewer with permission to cross. This, paired with the inconsistent density of road crossings,
could be why nearly no crossings are used in the final solution.

A richer linking method based on local topology and intersection context is needed to improve
the realism of simulating real-world informal crossing behavior as well as supporting a larger number of students.

Another limitation is computational performance: although runtime is acceptable for the current instance size,
the method needs further optimization to scale efficiently to larger student populations.
